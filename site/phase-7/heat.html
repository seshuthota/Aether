Heat and energy

A phone has no fan. Run the engine for five minutes and it heats up, the system lowers the clock, and the speed falls. This page shows two five-minute runs, how energy per token is worked out from the battery, and why the GPU, slower than the CPU, can be the better choice for a long chat.

What are we trying to do here?

A benchmark burst lasts a second or two. A person chatting runs the engine for minutes. On a phone those give different answers, because the chip is allowed to run fast only while it is cool. So bench_suite --sustain 300 decodes in 64-token bursts, back to back, for 300 seconds, and reports the speed of every 10-second window. With --monitor it also samples temperatures and battery power four times a second.

Two numbers come out: the sustained speed (tokens per second averaged over the whole run) and the energy per token in joules (J). A joule is one watt for one second. By the end you can work out energy per token from a power reading and a speed, and say what that number includes.

Why is the first minute faster?

Both runs below are Qwen2.5-0.5B Q4_0 on cores 2–7, on battery, each started cool (the second at 41.5 °C). The first is the engine at the end of Phase 7 (2026-10-21), the second after the speed work of 2026-10-29.

The newer engine does a 64-token burst from cold at 172.7 tok/s. What is its average over 5 minutes?

  • About 95 tok/s, a bit over half
    Within the first half minute the caps drop, and the rest of the run is at the lower clock.
  • About 170 tok/s; the work does not change
    The work is the same, but the clock it runs at is not.
  • About 30 tok/s; it overheats and nearly stops
    The system lowers the clock to hold the temperature, it does not stop the work.

Speed in each 10-second window measured. The newer engine is faster in almost every window, but both fall from their start. The cause is the clock limit, not the code: as the chip warms, the system lowers the highest frequency the cores may run at (clock caps). The newer run swings more, between 72 and 137; it did not record the caps, so which window ran at which clock is not known.

Takeaway: a phone runs fast only while cool, so a minutes-long average is the speed a long chat gets, and it is well below the first burst.

How do we measure energy per token?

Android reports the battery’s remaining charge through a charge counter. The drop in charge over a time window, times the battery voltage, gives the energy the phone drew; divided by the time, that is the average power in watts.

energy per token = power / speed
  Phase 7:  3.49 W / 85.7 tok/s = 0.041 J
  10-29:    2.65 W / 94.8 tok/s = 0.028 J
  • Watts are joules per second, and speed is tokens per second, so the seconds cancel and leave joules per token calculated.
  • The power is the whole phone: screen, radios and everything else, not just the CPU. The screen was kept on, because switching it off lowers the clock caps.
  • The counter moves in steps and covered 277 of the 300 seconds; a second estimate from sampled current and voltage gave 3.16 W (0.033 J). So the figure is good to about ±15 %.

Takeaway: energy per token is battery power divided by speed, and on a phone it counts everything that was switched on.

The phone draws 3 W while decoding at 100 tok/s. How many tokens per joule is that?

  • About 33
    3 W / 100 tok/s = 0.03 J per token, and 1 / 0.03 ≈ 33.
  • 300
    That multiplies power by speed; energy per token is power divided by speed.
  • 3
    That is the power; it needs dividing by the speed first.

Can a faster engine use less energy?

It can seem that a faster engine must work harder and so cost more. But most of a phone’s power is drawn whether a token finishes quickly or slowly: the screen, the memory, the cores running at their capped clock.

The newer engine is 11 % faster over 5 minutes. What happened to its energy per token?

  • It fell, by about a third
    Each token took less time, at about the same or lower power, so less energy went into it.
  • It rose: faster means more power
    Power is capped by the same thermal limit; the faster code gets more done within it.
  • It stayed the same: the work per token did not change
    The arithmetic per token did not change, but the time spent drawing power did.

From 0.041 to 0.028 J per token calculated, and the newer run also peaked cooler, 80.3 against 84.5 °C measured. The power reading fell too, from 3.49 to 2.65 W. Both runs were limited by the same thermal budget; the faster code simply finishes more tokens inside it.

Takeaway: on a phone, speed and energy usually improve together, because a token that finishes sooner spends less time drawing power.

CPU or GPU for a long chat?

After Phase 8 the engine can also decode on the phone’s GPU (Decoding on the GPU). The same test, 120 seconds each, 2026-10-29 measured:

CPU, cores 2–7157.64.55 W0.029 J88.8 °C
GPU102.72.17–2.48 W0.021–0.024 J67.6 °C

The GPU is slower, but it draws about half the power, so each token costs less energy, and the phone stays 20 °C cooler. Reading speed for a person is a few words per second, so 100 tok/s is already far faster than anyone reads. For a long answer the cooler, cheaper path can be the better one, and it leaves the CPU free for the app.

Takeaway: the fastest path is not always the best one on a phone; the GPU trades a third of the speed for a fifth to a quarter less energy per token and a much cooler phone.

An app shows a 50-token reply to a quick question. Which path makes more sense?

  • The CPU: the reply is short, so speed matters and heat has no time to build
    Over half a second the phone stays cool; the energy difference is tiny.
  • The GPU: it always uses less energy per token
    It does, but for 50 tokens the saving is a fraction of a joule, and the CPU answers sooner.
  • Either: speed and energy are the same for short replies
    Speed still differs (158 against 103 tok/s); only the heat does not have time to matter.

What you should take away

A phone is fast only while it is cool, so measure speed over minutes. Energy per token is battery power divided by speed, and it counts the whole phone. Faster code usually costs less energy per token, and the GPU, slower than the CPU, uses the least energy and runs much cooler.
